"Is my cycle normal?" is one of the most common questions anyone who tracks
their periods ends up asking, and it rarely has a single-number answer.
Cycle length varies from person to person, varies somewhat from cycle to
cycle in the same person, and — this is the part that surprises people — is
described with slightly different boundaries by different reputable medical
sources, because they're answering slightly different questions. This
article lays out what a typical range actually looks like, why the number
changes depending on where you look, what "irregular" does and does not
mean, and when a pattern in your own dates is worth bringing to a clinician
rather than just noting to yourself.

## How cycle length is counted

Cycle length is measured from the first day of full bleeding in one period
to the first day of full bleeding in the next — not the gap between periods,
and not the day light spotting begins if that's distinguishable from your
actual flow for you. Get this reference point wrong and every number that
follows is wrong by the same margin, which is worth being deliberate about
if you're measuring your own pattern rather than just estimating it from
memory. The [cycle-length calculator](/tools/cycle-length-calculator) on
this site exists specifically to do that measurement for you from a
handful of your own recent start dates, entirely in your browser.

## What a "typical" range actually looks like

The NHS describes an average menstrual cycle as being around 28 days, with
periods "ranging from every 21 days to every 35 days" as common variation
around that average — in other words, 21 to 35 days is the band the NHS
treats as ordinary rather than something to flag. You'll also see a
slightly wider band cited elsewhere in general health writing, commonly
somewhere around 24 to 38 days; the calculators on this site describe that
figure the same way. Neither number is wrong. They're both attempts to
summarize a genuinely continuous distribution of real people's cycles into
a single round-sounding range, and different sources draw the edges in
slightly different places depending on which population and which years of
reproductive life they're describing.

That last point matters more than it looks like it should. ACOG's guidance
aimed at people who have just started having periods gives a noticeably
wider figure: "the average menstrual cycle is about 28 days, but cycles
that are 21 to 45 days are also normal," and it adds that it can take six
years or more after a first period for a cycle to settle into a more
regular pattern. That's not a contradiction of the narrower adult ranges
above — it's a different question. The narrower ranges describe an
established reproductive-age pattern; the wider one describes a system in
its first several years of finding its own rhythm, when swings that would
be worth a second look in someone with ten years of steady cycles are still
just part of settling in.

## Cycle length isn't the same thing as period length

It's easy to conflate "how long my cycle is" with "how long I bleed for,"
and they answer different questions. The NHS describes a period itself —
the bleeding days, not the full cycle — as usually lasting somewhere
between 2 and 7 days, most commonly around 5, with the bleeding typically
heaviest in the first couple of days and lighter and browner toward the
end. A 28-day cycle with a 5-day period and a 35-day cycle with a 4-day
period are both unremarkable; period length and cycle length vary somewhat
independently of each other, and a change in one doesn't necessarily say
anything about the other. Worth flagging on its own: a period that
regularly runs longer than 7 days is one of the specific things the NHS
calls out as worth a GP conversation, covered again below.

## How this changes across reproductive life

The picture painted by a single "typical range" also shifts across a
lifetime, and it's worth knowing roughly where those shifts happen so a
change that's expected at one age isn't mistaken for a concerning one.
Periods, per the NHS, usually begin around age 12, though some start
meaningfully earlier or later without that delay being a cause for
concern on its own; most people have settled into a more regular pattern
by somewhere between 16 and 18, which lines up with ACOG's six-year
settling window from the section above. Cycles tend to stay comparatively
steady through the established reproductive years, then loosen up again as
the approach to menopause begins — usually somewhere between ages 45 and
55 — when cycles commonly become more variable, sometimes shorter,
sometimes longer, sometimes skipped, well before periods stop entirely.
Both ends of that timeline are described by the NHS as ordinary
variation tied to a specific, identifiable life stage, which is a
different situation from an unexplained change showing up in the middle
of an otherwise steady decade of cycles — that second kind of change is
the one worth mentioning to a clinician, covered in the section below.

resembles. It is not a coincidence that the boundary lands on the same 21
to 45 figure ACOG uses to describe cycles in the years just after a first
period — a validation bound for a calculator has to be wide enough to
accept a genuinely plausible human cycle at any life stage, including the
years when a cycle is least settled, even though the same number would be
a poor description of "typical" for someone with a decade of steady
28-day cycles behind them. A bound wide enough not to reject real people is
a different thing from a claim about what's average, and it's worth reading
it as exactly that.

## What "irregular" means — and what it doesn't

The NHS's own definition of irregular is specific: periods are irregular if
the gap between them is shorter than 21 days or longer than 35 days, or if
your own usual pattern changes noticeably. That's a per-cycle boundary
applied to an absolute number of days. The
[cycle-length calculator](/tools/cycle-length-calculator) on this site
answers a related but genuinely different question: it looks at the spread
between the shortest and longest of the handful of your own recent cycles
you type in, and labels that spread "regular" when it's 7 days or less,
"irregular" when it's wider. That 7-day figure is a plain, consumer-facing
description built into the arithmetic — not a clinical threshold, not a
diagnosis, and not a statement from anyone who has looked at your actual
history. It's a different axis from the NHS's definition: the calculator
is asking "how consistent are you with yourself," not "does any one of your
cycles fall outside a fixed number of days," and a pattern can score as
regular by one of these definitions while sitting close to the edge of the
other.

Both are useful, and neither is a verdict. The word "irregular," sitting
next to a real person's own dates, tends to land harder than it should —
three or four cycles is a short window to judge anything from, and a single
unusually early or late period is common enough that it can flip a
genuinely steady pattern to "irregular" by these definitions without
reflecting any lasting change in your body. Neither the NHS's absolute
boundary nor a calculator's variation figure knows your medical history,
knows why any particular cycle ran long or short, or can tell ordinary
month-to-month variation apart from something that would benefit from a
clinician's attention. Only a clinician working from a fuller picture than
a handful of dates can do that — which is exactly why the next two sections
exist.

## What actually causes normal variation

Cycle-to-cycle variation happens to nearly everyone to some degree, and the
NHS lists several ordinary contributors. Puberty and the approach of
menopause (usually between 45 and 55) are the two life stages where
irregularity is most expected, for the reasons in the section above.
Pregnancy is another — a missed or unusually late period is often the
first sign someone notices before any other test. Hormonal contraception
is a major one too: the progestogen-only pill, the contraceptive
injection, and an intrauterine system (IUS) can all change cycle length or
suppress a cycle's usual pattern entirely, which is a deliberate effect of
the method rather than a malfunction. Beyond those, losing or gaining a
significant amount of weight, ongoing stress and anxiety, and exercising
heavily enough to affect the body's energy balance are all recognized,
everyday contributors to a longer or shorter cycle than usual. Conditions
such as polycystic ovary syndrome and thyroid problems can also disrupt
cycle timing more persistently and are worth a clinician's attention if a
pattern doesn't settle on its own.

None of this is visible to a calculator's arithmetic, which only ever sees
the dates you give it. The mechanical reason
[predictions drift](/articles/how-period-predictions-work) away from what
a tracker expected is that real cycles respond to all of the above, and
the underlying method has no way to know any of it happened until enough
new dates pull the average toward what's actually going on. That's also
why a single wide gap, right after a stressful month or a bout of illness,
is rarely worth reading as a lasting change on its own — it's exactly the
kind of ordinary disruption this list describes, and it tends to resolve
itself the following cycle without ever needing to be flagged as anything
more.

## When to actually see a clinician

A single cycle landing outside a commonly cited range, on its own, is
rarely the useful signal — a pattern over time, or a specific accompanying
symptom, usually is. Based on the NHS's own guidance, it's worth talking to
a GP if:

- Your periods are irregular, and that's a change from your own usual
  pattern rather than how they've always been.
- A period lasts longer than 7 days.
- You have irregular periods together with other symptoms such as
  unexplained weight gain, persistent tiredness, new facial hair growth,
  or noticeably oily or dry skin — a combination the NHS specifically
  associates with hormonal causes worth investigating.
- You have bleeding between periods or after sex.
- Your periods stop for three months or longer and pregnancy, recent
  childbirth, or breastfeeding doesn't explain it.
- Your periods are irregular and you're finding it difficult to conceive
  — irregular cycles often mean irregular ovulation, which a clinician can
  help assess directly rather than by guesswork.

None of these require a "diagnosis" from a calculator first, and none of
them are things this site's tools, or any calculator, are built to weigh.
A calculator only ever does arithmetic on the dates you give it; a GP can
ask the follow-up questions, run the tests, and consider the fuller
context a web form structurally cannot.

## Bring the dates, not just the label

If a conversation with a clinician is where this is heading, raw dates are
more useful than a summary word. "Irregular" or "regular" flattens a
pattern into one label; the actual start dates of your last several
periods can show a clinician things a single average and a single spread
figure hide, like a gradual lengthening over many months or a cluster of
skipped cycles rather than one outlier. This is also where a period of
logging genuinely pays off over a one-off guess: an app that keeps every
