Clinicians typically describe the menstrual cycle in four phases:
menstrual, follicular, ovulatory, and luteal. LunaTrack uses those same
four names to colour its calendar and highlight where you are in your
cycle, which is exactly why it's worth understanding what each phase
actually involves before treating that colour as more than it is. This
article walks through what happens physically in each phase, in the order
they occur, and closes with the honesty point that matters most for
anyone using an app's calendar to think about it: a phase colour on a
calendar is calculated from dates, not measured from your actual hormone
levels that day.

## Menstrual phase

The menstrual phase is the one most people already recognize: it's your
period, the days your body sheds the uterine lining it built up in the
previous cycle because that cycle didn't result in a pregnancy. Cleveland
Clinic's overview of the menstrual cycle describes this as the point where
estrogen and progesterone levels are at their lowest, having dropped
enough to trigger the lining's release, and notes that this phase
typically lasts a handful of days — commonly cited as somewhere in the
range of three to seven days, though the exact length varies by person and
even cycle to cycle for the same person. By convention, day one of a new
cycle is counted as the first day of full bleeding, which is also the
reference point every calculator and every phase estimate on this site
uses.

Cramping is common during this phase for many people, driven by the
uterus contracting to help shed its lining — if that's a symptom worth
tracking for you specifically, and how to tell an ordinary pattern from
one worth a clinician's attention, [which symptoms are actually worth
tracking](/articles/what-symptoms-are-worth-tracking) covers that in more
depth than this article does.

It's worth being precise about one overlap that trips people up: the
menstrual phase and the follicular phase both start on day one of a
cycle, and by most descriptions the menstrual phase is really the opening
stretch of the follicular phase rather than something entirely separate
from it — follicle development is already quietly under way in the
ovaries while the uterine lining is still shedding. The two are usually
described as separate phases anyway because they're doing such visibly
different things: one is about the lining coming down, the other is about
the next egg being prepared.

Two things are worth knowing before diving into the four phases
individually. First, the boundaries between them aren't as crisp in real
biology as four labelled blocks on a calendar suggest — hormone levels
rise and fall on continuous curves, and a "phase" is a convenient way of
describing a stretch of that curve, not a switch that flips at midnight.
Second, most descriptions of a "typical" cycle, including the rough day
numbers used in this article, are illustrated against a 28-day cycle
purely because it's a common average and an easy one to picture — your own
cycle, at whatever length it actually runs, moves through the same four
phases in the same order, just with the timing of each stretched or
compressed to fit.

## Follicular phase

The follicular phase technically overlaps with the start of the menstrual
phase — both are counted from day one — but it extends well beyond it,
running from the first day of a period until ovulation. During this
stretch, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) rises and triggers a group of
follicles in the ovaries to begin developing, each containing an
immature egg; over the following days, one of those follicles typically
becomes dominant and continues maturing while the others recede. As it
develops, estrogen levels rise, and that rising estrogen is what rebuilds
the uterine lining that was shed during the period, preparing it for a
possible pregnancy later in the cycle.

This is also, notably, the least predictable phase length-wise. Cleveland
Clinic's overview defines this phase by its boundaries — it begins on the
day a period starts and ends at ovulation — without saying which part of
a cycle absorbs the month-to-month variation, so that next point is worth
attributing to where it actually belongs: it's the working assumption
behind this site's own calculators, not a claim from either cited source.
LunaTrack's [prediction arithmetic](/articles/how-period-predictions-work)
holds the luteal phase fixed at close to fourteen days and counts backward
from a projected period to estimate ovulation, precisely because that
arithmetic only makes sense if a longer or shorter cycle is mostly the
follicular phase running longer or shorter, not the luteal phase doing so.
That's an assumption this site's calculators rely on, not a measurement,
and it's a different — and more specific — claim than anything Cleveland
Clinic's overview states about the phase itself.

## Ovulatory phase

Ovulation is the release of a mature egg from the ovary, triggered by a
sharp rise in luteinizing hormone (LH) — often called the LH surge — which
causes the dominant follicle to rupture and release its egg into the
fallopian tube. Cleveland Clinic's overview of ovulation notes that the
released egg survives roughly 12 to 24 hours before the body reabsorbs it
if it isn't fertilized in that window, which is a much narrower window
than most people expect from an event often associated with an entire
"fertile week." The window feels wider in practice mainly because of
sperm, not the egg: sperm can survive inside the body for several days
waiting for ovulation to happen, which is why a fertile window is
described as a multi-day span even though the egg itself is only viable
for less than a day.

Some people notice physical signs around ovulation — Cleveland Clinic
lists breast tenderness, bloating, pelvic or abdominal discomfort, light
spotting, and changes in libido or mood as things some people experience,
though far from everyone notices any of them, and none are a reliable
signal on their own. The LH surge itself is what an ovulation test (OPK)
strip detects directly, which is why a positive OPK result reflects
something that's actually happening in your body that day, rather than
an estimate counted backward from a projected future date the way a
calendar marker is.

Because a cycle's length variability lives almost entirely in the
follicular phase before it, ovulation timing shifts around with the rest
of the cycle rather than falling on a fixed day — it typically lands
roughly midway through an individual's own cycle, but "midway through a
28-day cycle" and "midway through a 35-day cycle" are different calendar
dates entirely. This is the phase LunaTrack's calendar marks most
cautiously: the ovulatory marker and fertile-window highlight are only
shown once there's enough of your own logged history to make a reasonably
confident estimate, and even then the marker is a projection, not a
measurement of an egg actually being released on that day. Basal body
temperature charting and ovulation test (OPK/LH) results, both available
in [LunaTrack's tracking features](/features), can corroborate that a
calendar estimate lines up with what's actually happening in your body
that cycle — they reflect a real physical signal rather than only an
assumption baked into the arithmetic, which is worth knowing if the
fertile window matters to you specifically.

## Luteal phase

After ovulation, the ruptured follicle transforms into a temporary
structure called the corpus luteum, which produces progesterone. That
progesterone further prepares the uterine lining to potentially support a
pregnancy, and it's also responsible for many of the physical and
emotional changes some people notice in the days before a period — the
timing [premenstrual symptoms](/articles/what-symptoms-are-worth-tracking)
most often cluster in. If the egg released during ovulation isn't
fertilized, the corpus luteum breaks down after a fairly consistent
stretch of time, progesterone and estrogen levels fall, and that drop
triggers the uterine lining to shed — which begins the menstrual phase of
the next cycle, closing the loop.

The luteal phase is, notably, the most stable-length part of the entire
cycle, commonly cited as lasting close to fourteen days regardless of how
long the rest of the cycle runs. That figure isn't incidental to this
site: it's the same fourteen-day assumption LunaTrack's own calendar uses
when it counts backward from a projected next period to estimate an
ovulation day, described in full in [how period predictions actually
work](/articles/how-period-predictions-work). It's a population average
rather than a guarantee about any individual's own luteal phase, which is
exactly why an ovulation estimate built on it is an estimate and not a
measurement.

## Why the four phases matter beyond fertility

It's easy to read a four-phase framework as being only about fertility,
since ovulation is the phase most tracking apps make the most visual
fuss over. But the same framework is useful for making sense of
non-fertility patterns too, because the hormonal swings described above
don't only affect the reproductive system. The drop in estrogen and
progesterone that defines the menstrual phase, the rise in estrogen
through the follicular phase, and the rise and later fall of progesterone
through the luteal phase are the same hormonal shifts widely associated
with the physical and emotional symptoms covered in [which symptoms are
worth tracking](/articles/what-symptoms-are-worth-tracking) — which is
exactly why a symptom pattern so often lines up with a particular phase
rather than showing up at random points in a cycle. Knowing which phase a
symptom tends to cluster in doesn't change the symptom, but it does turn
"I feel awful sometimes" into "I feel this way in a specific, recurring
part of my cycle," which is a more useful thing to notice about yourself
and a more useful thing to tell a clinician.

## What the calendar colours are, and are not

Here's the point worth carrying away from all four phases above: a phase
label on LunaTrack's calendar, or on any period-tracking calendar built
the same way, is calculated from your logged or typed dates using the
pattern this article just walked through — it is never a direct
measurement of your hormone levels, your ovaries, or anything happening
inside your body on that specific day. Two people the calendar labels as
"probably in the luteal phase" today could be in quite different real
biological places if either of their actual cycles is running longer or
shorter than their own recent average, because the whole estimate rests on
that average holding steady this time. This isn't a flaw specific to
LunaTrack; it's true of calendar-based phase estimation generally, and
it's worth being explicit about rather than letting a confident-looking
colour on a calendar imply more certainty than calendar arithmetic can
honestly offer.

None of this makes the four-phase framework useless — understanding which
phase you're estimated to be in is still a reasonable way to make sense of
where in your cycle a given symptom, mood shift, or fertility sign is
likely to sit, and it's the same framework a clinician will use if you
ever discuss your cycle with one. It's simply worth treating a calendar's
phase colour as what it is: a reasonable estimate built from your own
averages, the same way the [ovulation calculator](/tools/ovulation-calculator)
on this site openly counts backward from a projected period using the
fourteen-day assumption above, rather than a measurement of anything that
has already happened in your body this cycle.

The more your own logged history reflects reality — consistent flow
logging so start dates are accurate, and enough cycles behind the average
for it to mean something — the more that estimate is worth trusting as a
rough guide. It never stops being an estimate, but a rough guide grounded
in several of your own real cycles is a meaningfully better one than a
single textbook 28-day illustration like the one this article has used
throughout. If your own cycle length varies enough that you're not sure
how much weight to put on any single phase estimate, [what cycle length
is normal](/articles/what-cycle-length-is-normal) covers how to read that
variation, and the [cycle-length calculator](/tools/cycle-length-calculator)
will show you the actual spread behind your own recent cycles rather than
an assumption borrowed from an average person.
