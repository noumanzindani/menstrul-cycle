Appointments about your periods are often short, and "my periods have
been bad lately" doesn't give a clinician much to work with in whatever
time there is. Concrete dates, a described
pattern, and specific details do. This article covers what's actually
useful to bring to that conversation and why, and maps it directly onto
LunaTrack's shipped doctor-summary PDF export — including two things worth
knowing plainly before you rely on it, because a document you might print
or hand to someone else deserves an honest account of what it leaves out
as much as what it includes.

## What "heavy" and "painful" actually mean to a clinician

Vague words like "heavy" and "painful" mean different things to different
people, so a clinician generally wants the specifics behind them rather
than the adjective itself. For heavy bleeding, the NHS gives a set of
concrete markers rather than a subjective impression: needing to change a
pad or tampon every one to two hours, needing to use two period products
together, periods lasting more than seven days, passing clots larger than
about 2.5cm (roughly the size of a coin), bleeding through to clothes or
bedding, avoiding normal activities because of your period, or feeling
tired or short of breath as a result. Any of those, and how often they
happen, is far more useful to report than "my periods are heavy."

Pain works the same way. The NHS describes ordinary period pain as
cramping that starts around or just before a period and typically lasts
up to about three days — a pattern worth being able to compare your own
against. ACOG's guidance on dysmenorrhea draws a distinction that matters
even more here: primary dysmenorrhea is highest on the first day of a
period and eases as bleeding continues, while secondary dysmenorrhea —
caused by an underlying condition such as endometriosis, fibroids, or
adenomyosis — tends to worsen over time, can start before a period begins,
and may not resolve when bleeding ends. Being able to describe which shape
your own pain follows, with dates, is exactly the kind of detail that
turns a vague complaint into something a clinician can act on. [Which
symptoms are worth tracking](/articles/what-symptoms-are-worth-tracking)
covers how to log that pattern day by day so it's actually visible by the
time an appointment comes around, rather than reconstructed from memory
the morning of.

## What a clinician is actually trying to rule in or out

Concrete detail matters partly because it points a clinician toward a
specific next step rather than a generic one. Heavy periods, per the NHS,
are sometimes just normal for a particular life stage — around the time
periods first start, after a pregnancy, or approaching menopause — but
they can also be caused by conditions affecting the womb or ovaries such
as fibroids, endometriosis, or polycystic ovary syndrome, by conditions
that affect how easily blood clots, by certain medications including some
anticoagulants, or, rarely, by something more serious. Which of those is
worth investigating first depends heavily on the details you can provide:
how long the pattern has been going on, whether it came with other
symptoms, and how it compares to your own history rather than to a
general population average. Painful periods work the same way — primary
dysmenorrhea is usually managed first with anti-inflammatory painkillers
or hormonal contraception, while a pattern that looks more like secondary
dysmenorrhea may lead to an ultrasound or a specialist referral to look
for an underlying cause. None of that triage happens well from a vague
description; it happens well from dates, frequency, and a described
pattern.

It's also worth knowing when the NHS's guidance points toward urgent
rather than routine care: pelvic or period pain that is severe, or clearly
worse than usual, and hasn't responded to painkillers is listed as a
reason to seek an urgent GP appointment or call NHS 111 rather than
waiting for a routine slot. A dated log doesn't replace that judgment call
— it's still worth acting on severe or unusual pain right away rather than
waiting to log it first.

## Why raw dates beat a summary word

The same principle that applies to a single cycle-length calculation
applies here at a larger scale: a clinician can see things in a list of
actual dates — a gradual lengthening, a cluster of unusually heavy months,
a pain pattern that's shifted from early-peaking to persistent — that a
single summary word like "irregular" or "heavy" flattens away entirely.
[What cycle length is normal](/articles/what-cycle-length-is-normal) covers
this same idea from the angle of a single calculator result; the doctor
summary described below is the version of that principle built for an
actual appointment, covering more than cycle length alone.

## What LunaTrack's doctor-summary export actually contains

LunaTrack can generate a PDF summary of your cycle and symptom history
from the [insights section of the app](/features), built entirely from
data already on your device, and hand it to your phone's normal share
sheet — from there, where it goes is entirely up to you. The export
includes several sections, each pulled straight from your own logged
history rather than written fresh for the document:

- **A profile block**, if you've filled any of it in — your age, height,
  current weight, and age at your first period, each shown only if you've
  actually answered it, with a calculated BMI figure included when both
  height and weight are on file. Skip all of it and this block simply
  doesn't appear; there's no blank heading left behind.
- **Cycle statistics** — how many cycles have been tracked, your average
  cycle length, your shortest and longest recent cycle, and how many days
  it's been since your last period began.
- **A recent-cycles table** — start date, cycle length, and period length
  for your most recent cycles, capped at twelve so the document stays a
  readable length rather than growing forever for a long-time user.
- **A cycle-patterns summary**, when there's enough history for one — a
  few plain-language observations, such as a recent trend toward a longer
  or shorter cycle, described descriptively rather than as a diagnosis.
- **A weight trend**, if you track weight, reported in kilograms
  regardless of which unit you normally view it in on your phone, since
  this is meant to read as a clinical document rather than a personal
  screen.
- **An estimated-fertility section**, if you have enough history for a
  confident estimate, explicitly labelled as a calendar-based estimate and
  noted as not confirmed by ovulation testing.
- **Symptom and mood frequency** — a count of how often each logged
  symptom and mood has appeared, so a clinician can see which ones recur
  often rather than only which ones you happen to remember.
- **A "notes to discuss" section**, when the app's own insights have
  flagged anything worth a second look — short, plain-language prompts
  rather than a diagnosis, meant to make sure something the app noticed
  in your history doesn't go unmentioned just because you forgot it was
  there.

None of it is invented for the document — it's the same underlying data
and the same [cycle predictions](/articles/how-period-predictions-work)
described elsewhere on this site, laid out for a clinician to skim
quickly rather than scroll through inside the app.

## What it deliberately leaves out

Two specific exclusions are worth stating plainly, because they're the
kind of thing a reader would otherwise have to just assume one way or the
other, and this document is exactly the kind of sensitive, potentially
printed-or-emailed record where that assumption matters.

**Photos are never included.** If you also keep a photo timeline in
LunaTrack, none of it appears anywhere in the doctor-summary export — not
as a thumbnail, not as a reference, not as an attachment. This isn't a
setting you have to remember to turn off; there is no version of this
export that includes an image, full stop.

**Sexual activity is excluded by default.** Sexual activity is logged as
its own distinct category, kept separate from the general symptom list,
and it's left out of the doctor-summary export by default in the same way.
If it's relevant to the conversation you're having — pain during sex is
one of the specific things worth mentioning under period pain above, for
example — that's something worth raising out loud during the appointment
even though the export itself won't carry it for you.

Both of these are deliberate defaults for a document that's likely to be
printed, emailed, or handed to someone else, and it's worth reading them
as a narrower promise on purpose, not as something the export merely
happens not to do today.

## How to actually prepare for the appointment

A few practical habits make the export, and the appointment itself, more
useful. Generate the PDF a day or two ahead of time rather than in the
waiting room, so you have a chance to read through it yourself first and
notice anything that looks off or anything you'd want to add verbally.
Bring the raw list of your last several period start dates as well as the
export itself, especially if the appointment is about a change in pattern
rather than a routine check-in — a clinician asking a follow-up question
about a specific date is easier to answer from your own notes than from
memory alone. If something relevant isn't in the export by design, such as
sexual activity relevant to pain timing, plan to mention it directly
rather than assuming the document covers everything you want to discuss.
And write down two or three specific questions ahead of time — a short
appointment goes further when you're not trying to remember what you meant
to ask while you're in it.

It's also worth deciding in advance what you actually want out of the
appointment, because that shapes which part of the summary matters most.
If the concern is pain, the recent-cycles table and the symptom-frequency
section together are what let a clinician judge whether your pattern
looks more like the early-peaking primary shape or the worsening
secondary one described above. If the concern is a change in bleeding, the
cycle statistics and recent-cycles table are the most relevant sections,
and it's worth mentioning any of the NHS's specific heavy-bleeding markers
you recognize in your own experience even if you haven't been logging them
as a named symptom. If nothing specific is prompting the visit and it's
simply a routine check-in, the full export as generated is normally enough
on its own.

## When this kind of summary matters most

A dated, specific summary is most useful exactly when the conversation is
about a pattern rather than a single event: a heavy-bleeding pattern
matching several of the NHS's concrete markers above, a pain pattern that
seems to be shifting from the early-peaking primary shape toward something
that lingers or worsens, or a cycle-length pattern that's changed
noticeably from what used to be typical for you — covered in more depth in
[what cycle length is normal](/articles/what-cycle-length-is-normal). It's
just as reasonable to bring one to a routine check-in with nothing
specific prompting it; a clinician generally welcomes concrete history
over none.

If cycle length itself is part of what's uncertain going into the
appointment, the [cycle-length calculator](/tools/cycle-length-calculator)
on this site is a quick way to see your own recent average and spread
before you go, and LunaTrack's full [feature set](/features) covers
everything else the app tracks toward the same summary over the longer
term.

## A summary is only as good as the log behind it

None of the sections above can describe a pattern that was never logged
in the first place. A doctor-summary export generated the week you decide
something feels off will still only cover whatever history exists by
then — which is a reasonable argument for logging consistently well
before you think you'll need it, rather than starting the week of a
concerning appointment. [Which symptoms are worth
tracking](/articles/what-symptoms-are-worth-tracking) covers which
categories are worth that ongoing effort and which aren't, so the summary
this article describes actually has something substantial behind it by
the time you need to generate one. A single export, read once, is useful.
Months of consistent logging behind that export is what makes it worth
trusting.
