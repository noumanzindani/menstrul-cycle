Open a period-tracking app for the first time and it can feel like it wants
everything: dozens of symptom chips, a mood picker, temperature, discharge,
sleep. Logging all of it every day is exhausting, and exhausting habits get
abandoned within a couple of cycles — at which point you have no pattern at
all, just a few weeks of dense data followed by silence. A shorter, more
consistent log beats a comprehensive one you quit in month two. This
article is about which symptoms actually carry signal worth the taps, why,
and how to log them so a real pattern can show up in a few cycles — mapped
specifically to [what LunaTrack tracks](/features) today, not a longer
wish-list of categories.

## Signal versus noise

A symptom is worth tracking when three things are true: it recurs often
enough that a pattern is even possible to see, it's specific enough that
you can log it the same way each time rather than reinventing the
description, and it's the kind of thing you or a clinician would actually
act on if a pattern turned up. A lot of what a maximalist symptom list
offers fails at least one of those. A vague daily "how do you feel"
rating, logged from memory at the end of a long day, mixes cycle effects
with everything else that happened that day — work stress, poor sleep, an
argument — and the mixing makes the cycle-linked part harder to see, not
easier. A specific, recurring, nameable symptom, logged on the day it
happens, is what actually accumulates into something readable across
cycles.

## Flow itself is the anchor everything else reads against

Before any symptom pattern means anything, the flow log underneath it has
to be consistent, because every phase estimate and every cycle-length
figure on this site is measured from the days you mark as bleeding.
LunaTrack's daily log and its "period ended" toggle exist specifically so
that boundary is unambiguous rather than inferred — marking the day flow
actually stops is what lets the app (and you, looking back later) tell a
short, light period from a period that's still ongoing but very light on
its last day. Skipping this part, or logging it inconsistently, doesn't
just cost you a flow record: it quietly degrades every symptom pattern
layered on top of it, since "three days before my period" only means
anything if the app knows reliably when each period actually started.
This is the one category on this list that's worth logging every single
day without exception, even on days when nothing else seems worth noting.

## The physical and emotional symptoms most people recognize

LunaTrack's symptom and mood logging covers a defined set of options
rather than a free-text mood essay, and that constraint is deliberate: the
same label used consistently across months is what makes a pattern visible
at all. Many of the most commonly reported symptoms map closely to what
the NHS describes under premenstrual syndrome (PMS) — the physical and
emotional changes that show up in the weeks before a period for many
people who menstruate. The NHS lists mood swings, feeling low or
irritable, feeling anxious or emotionally sensitive, tiredness or trouble
sleeping, bloating or cramping, breast tenderness, headaches, and changes
in appetite or cravings as common PMS symptoms, adding that each person's
pattern differs and can vary from month to month. Cramps, headache,
bloating, tender breasts, fatigue, and mood options like irritable,
anxious, sensitive, and low are exactly the kind of categories worth
logging for this reason: not because any single occurrence means
something, but because a symptom that shows up in the same stretch of
several consecutive cycles — say, consistently in the days leading into a
period — is a pattern worth having on record, both for your own planning
and for a conversation with a clinician if it's affecting your daily life.
That timing detail is worth understanding on its own terms; [how the four
cycle phases actually work](/articles/cycle-phases-explained) covers what's
happening physiologically in that stretch before a period, which is why
symptoms often cluster there for people who experience PMS.

Logging these consistently pays off in a very practical way, too. The NHS's
own advice for PMS is to try lifestyle changes first — regular exercise, a
balanced diet with smaller, more frequent meals, enough sleep, stress
reduction such as yoga or meditation, and over-the-counter pain relief like
ibuprofen or paracetamol where appropriate — and to see a GP if those
changes don't help or if symptoms are affecting daily life. A GP hearing
"I feel awful sometimes" has very little to work with; a GP looking at
which specific symptoms recur, on which days relative to your period, over
several logged cycles, has something they can actually reason about and
suggest a next step for.

## Period pain: the pattern matters more than the fact of it

Nearly everyone who has periods experiences some pain around them at some
point, so "I get cramps" on its own isn't especially informative to a
clinician — the shape of the pattern is what's useful, and that's exactly
what a dated log can show that a memory can't. The NHS describes ordinary
period pain as cramping in the lower abdomen that can spread to the back
and thighs, typically starting around or just before a period and lasting
up to about three days. ACOG's guidance on dysmenorrhea — the clinical term
for period pain — draws a distinction that's directly useful for logging:
primary dysmenorrhea is cramping caused by prostaglandins made in the
uterine lining, is highest on the first day of a period, and tends to ease
as bleeding continues; secondary dysmenorrhea, by contrast, is caused by an
underlying condition such as endometriosis, fibroids, or adenomyosis, tends
to get worse over time rather than following that early-peak pattern, can
begin before a period starts, and may not resolve when bleeding ends.
Those are two different shapes, and the difference is exactly what a
day-by-day symptom log makes visible where a single "period pain: yes"
entry cannot. Logging cramps, pelvic pain, and back pain as the specific,
same-named symptoms each day they occur — rather than a single end-of-cycle
summary — is what lets you or a clinician later see which of those two
shapes your own pattern actually follows.

It's also worth logging pain severity in relative terms over time, even
informally, because the NHS's own guidance on when period pain needs
medical attention is itself pattern-based: it recommends seeing a GP if
periods become more painful, heavier, or irregular than they used to be,
if pain is severe enough to stop your usual daily activities, or if you
notice pain during sex or when urinating or passing a bowel movement — and
it recommends urgent care specifically if pain is severe or clearly worse
than usual and painkillers haven't helped. Every one of those is a
comparison to your own baseline, which is exactly what a consistent log
gives you and a single memory of "it hurt a lot this month" does not.

## Fertility-awareness signs: useful, but only as corroboration

LunaTrack also offers basal body temperature (BBT) charting and a place to
record ovulation test (OPK/LH) results, both framed on the features page
as awareness tools rather than diagnostic ones. These carry a different
kind of signal from a symptom chip: they're a direct physical measurement
or test result on a specific day, rather than a subjective symptom, and
a positive OPK result or a sustained temperature shift can corroborate a
calendar-based estimate of ovulation rather than standing alone. Neither
is a method of contraception, and neither turns an estimate into a
certainty — they're worth logging specifically because they add a second,
independent kind of evidence to a fertility estimate that would otherwise
rest on cycle-length arithmetic alone.

## Notes: the category for everything a chip can't capture

Alongside flow, symptoms, mood, BBT, and OPK results, LunaTrack's daily
log includes a plain notes field, and it's worth treating as a real
tracking category rather than an afterthought. Some genuinely useful
context doesn't fit a predefined chip at all — starting a new medication,
an unusually stressful week at work, travel across time zones, a change
in exercise routine — and every one of those is exactly the kind of
outside factor that can explain an otherwise puzzling symptom or cycle
change later. A short note the day something unusual happens is often
more useful in hindsight than trying to remember, months later, whether
anything out of the ordinary was going on that cycle.

## What's usually not worth forcing

A few things are worth being deliberate about not over-logging. Symptoms
you're not actually experiencing but feel like you "should" check because
they're on the list add noise, not signal — an unchecked box should mean
"this didn't happen," and ticking something out of habit rather than
observation undermines that. Retroactively guessing at a symptom's
severity days later, once you can no longer remember clearly, is a similar
trap: the value of same-day logging is that it captures what actually
happened rather than a reconstructed impression of the week. And no
combination of symptoms, temperature readings, or test results should be
read as telling you which days are or aren't likely to result in
pregnancy with any certainty — that framing belongs to contraceptive
methods that have been studied for that purpose, not to a calendar
estimate corroborated by a chart.

## Logging usefully: consistency over completeness

A few habits make the difference between a symptom log that shows a
pattern and one that's just noise. Log on the day something happens,
rather than reconstructing a week from memory at the end of it — same-day
entries are more accurate and take less mental effort each time. Use the
same label every time a symptom recurs rather than switching between
similar-sounding descriptions ("cramps" one day and "tummy ache" the next
for the same sensation), since a pattern is only visible if the labels
actually line up across cycles. Expect to need two or three full cycles
before a real pattern is visible at all — the NHS's own advice for people
managing PMS is to keep a symptom diary for at least two menstrual cycles
before bringing it to a GP, which is a reasonable minimum for almost any
symptom pattern, not just PMS specifically. And when it's time to discuss
any of this with a clinician, bring the dated log itself rather than a
verbal summary of it; [preparing a doctor-ready cycle
summary](/articles/preparing-a-doctor-ready-cycle-summary) covers exactly
how to turn that log into something useful for an appointment.

## Where this fits with the rest of your cycle

Symptom timing only makes sense in the context of where you are in your
cycle, and that context is itself an estimate — worth understanding for
what it actually is before treating any of this as more precise than it
can be. A symptom that reliably shows up "a few days before my period"
is only a meaningful pattern if your period dates themselves are
consistent enough to measure against; if your cycle length swings widely
from month to month, the same symptom might land anywhere from a week
before a period to right at its start, which can look like an
inconsistent symptom when the real variability is in the cycle length
underneath it. If you're trying to work out whether your own cycle is
steady enough that a symptom pattern is even a fair comparison across
recent cycles, the [cycle-length calculator](/tools/cycle-length-calculator)
is a reasonable place to check that first, and [what cycle length is
normal](/articles/what-cycle-length-is-normal) covers how much variation is
ordinary before treating any single wide gap as a red flag on its own.

None of this requires logging perfectly from day one. A log that starts
today, kept consistently for the categories that actually matter to you,
is worth more three cycles from now than an ambitious system abandoned
after a week. Start with flow, whichever symptoms recur often enough to
notice, and a note whenever something out of the ordinary happens — the
rest can be added later if it turns out to matter.
